author: J. Okafor and L. Brandt
date: Department of Psychology, Northfield University
abstract: We examined whether the amount of sleep students get the night before a test predicts how much they remember. Eighty-four undergraduates kept sleep diaries for two weeks and completed a word-recall task each morning. Students who slept seven hours or more recalled about 18 percent more words than those who slept fewer than six, an effect that held after controlling for caffeine intake and time of day.

# Introduction

Sleep is widely thought to consolidate memory, yet many students treat it as the first thing to give up before an exam.
Laboratory studies show clear effects of total sleep deprivation on recall,[^1] but fewer studies have looked at the
ordinary, partial sleep loss that students actually experience during term.

This paper asks a simple question: on an ordinary morning, does last night's sleep predict how well a student remembers
what they studied the evening before?

# Method

## Participants

Eighty-four undergraduates (52 women, 32 men; mean age 20.1) took part for course credit. None reported a diagnosed
sleep disorder.

## Procedure

Each evening, participants studied a list of 30 unrelated nouns for five minutes. The next morning they wrote down as
many as they could remember and recorded when they had gone to bed, when they had woken and how much caffeine they had
had.

# Results

Recall rose steadily with sleep duration (@tbl:recall). The difference between the shortest and longest sleepers was
significant, and remained so when caffeine and testing time were added to the model.

| Hours of sleep | Nights | Words recalled |
| :------------- | -----: | -------------: |
| Fewer than 6   |    212 |           11.4 |
| 6 to 7         |    489 |           12.6 |
| 7 or more      |    475 |           13.5 |
