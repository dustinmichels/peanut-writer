abstract: This study examined whether exposure to natural morning light improves sustained attention during the workday. Sixty office workers completed a vigilance task at 10 a.m. and 3 p.m. on days when they did or did not spend 20 minutes outdoors before work. Morning light was associated with fewer missed signals in the afternoon session, suggesting a modest but practical benefit.

# Introduction

Many office workers spend most of their waking hours indoors under artificial lighting. Exposure to bright light early
in the day helps set the body's internal clock, and some evidence suggests it also supports alertness later on. Few
studies, however, have tested this in ordinary working conditions rather than in the laboratory.

The present study asked whether a short period outdoors before work would improve performance on an afternoon attention
task. We expected that participants would miss fewer signals on mornings when they had been outside.

# Method

## Participants

Sixty adults (34 women, 26 men) aged 24 to 58 years were recruited from three offices in the same city. All worked
regular daytime hours and none reported shift work during the previous year.

## Materials and Procedure

Participants completed a 10-minute vigilance task twice a day for four weeks. On alternate weeks they were asked to
spend 20 minutes outdoors between waking and arriving at work. Light exposure was recorded with a wrist-worn sensor.
